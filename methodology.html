Methodology

eight phases, start to finish

Every engagement runs the same eight phases in order, regardless of scope. This is the public summary of that process — the full methodology document, with per-phase checklists and procedures, is delivered as an engagement artifact, not published here.

The eight phases

  1. 01

    Pre-engagement and authorization

    Establish scope, testing windows, exclusions, escalation contacts, and an emergency stop procedure — all in writing. Nothing is tested until a signed statement of work, rules of engagement, and authorization letter are in place.

  2. 02

    Passive reconnaissance

    Build a picture of the target's public footprint without touching it directly. DNS history, certificate transparency logs, public code, and personnel exposure are reviewed to understand what's discoverable before a single packet is sent.

  3. 03

    Active reconnaissance

    Confirm what's actually live and reachable within the authorized scope. This is the first direct contact with in-scope systems, kept within agreed testing windows and rate limits to avoid unnecessary load or disruption.

  4. 04

    Enumeration

    Turn what's reachable into a detailed inventory — exposed services, endpoints, accounts, and application surface. This is the map that vulnerability analysis works from, so it's built systematically rather than sampled.

  5. 05

    Vulnerability analysis

    Identify candidate weaknesses across the mapped surface, both known issue classes and logic or configuration flaws found by manual inspection. Every candidate is triaged for plausibility before it's carried into testing.

  6. 06

    Exploitation

    Confirm that a candidate weakness is real by demonstrating it, not by assuming it. Testing is non-destructive by default and stops once impact is proven — there's no reason to push past the point that establishes the finding.

  7. 07

    Post-exploitation

    Establish what a confirmed foothold actually means — what data, systems, or privileges it exposes, and how far it could reach. This is assessed and documented, not driven to disruption.

  8. 08

    Reporting

    Deliver findings a technical team can reproduce and a non-technical stakeholder can act on — reproduction steps, evidence, a severity score, and remediation guidance for each, plus an executive summary of overall risk.

Standards alignment

what each contributes
PTES
Penetration Testing Execution Standard — the phase structure above is built on it, from pre-engagement through reporting.
OWASP WSTG v4.2
Web Security Testing Guide — the coverage checklist for web application engagements, so nothing gets skipped by accident.
NIST SP 800-115
Technical Guide to Information Security Testing and Assessment — grounds the network and infrastructure testing approach.
CVSS v3.1
Common Vulnerability Scoring System — the scale every finding is scored against, so severity means the same thing report to report.

Scoring discipline

Findings are scored on demonstrated, verified impact against your environment — not on a scanner's default rating and not on how serious an issue could theoretically be in someone else's. Rosec does not inflate severity to make a report look more valuable; a low finding is reported as a low finding.

Safety

Testing is non-destructive by default. Anything destructive — a test that could crash a service, corrupt data, or cause an outage — requires explicit written authorization before it's attempted, separate from the general scope agreement.

If real personal data is exposed at any point during testing, testing halts immediately and you're notified right away — that notification isn't held for the final report.

Finding identifiers

Every finding is assigned an identifier in the form ROSEC-2026-014 at initial report and keeps that identifier through remediation and retest. It's how a finding stays traceable from the day it's reported to the day it's confirmed closed.
