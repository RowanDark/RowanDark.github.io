Web application assessment — fintech SaaS platform

5 findings — 1 critical, 2 high, 0 medium, 1 low, 1 info

A Swiss fintech SaaS platform engaged Rosec for a web application penetration test of its customer-facing account and payments portal. Testing surfaced a chain starting from a High cross-site request forgery issue: because state-changing requests weren't bound to a verified session, an attacker could combine it with a High stored cross-site scripting flaw in a user-editable content field to trigger unauthorized actions on a victim's behalf. Separately, the payment initiation flow trusted the transaction amount supplied by the client rather than revalidating it server-side — a Critical finding, since it meant the amount actually charged could be manipulated in transit. Two lower-severity issues rounded out the report: a Low session token with a lifetime far longer than the sensitivity of the data it protected justified, and an Info analytics integration configured to capture more user data than the stated privacy policy accounted for.

The client addressed the chain first: CSRF tokens on every state-changing request plus output encoding on the vulnerable content field closed both ends of it in the same release. Amount validation was moved server-side and made authoritative, session lifetime was cut to match the sensitivity of the account data, and the analytics configuration was scoped down to exclude the unintended fields. Rosec ran a full remediation retest against each original finding individually — all five were confirmed fixed, with no exceptions carried forward.

External attack surface assessment — mid-market services firm

7 findings — 0 critical, 1 high, 2 medium, 2 low, 2 info

A mid-market services firm engaged Rosec for a standalone external attack surface assessment ahead of a funding round's technical due diligence — no active testing, just a passive map of what the organization actually exposed to the internet. Enumeration surfaced a handful of forgotten subdomains from a discontinued product line, still resolving and still reachable years after the product was retired. Two of those pointed at non-production staging environments that were never meant to be internet-facing at all, one of which — a High finding — was running a web server version with known, unpatched vulnerabilities well past its end-of-life date. Several other hosts returned service banners disclosing exact patch levels, letting an attacker fingerprint exploitable versions without sending a single crafted request — a Medium finding repeated across two hosts. The remainder were Low and Info items: expired-but-still-listed DNS records and a handful of subdomains with more verbose configuration than necessary.

None of this surfaced through the client's own inventory — the forgotten subdomains and staging environments weren't in any asset list, because nobody still working there had provisioned them. That was the actual finding: the organization's real internet-facing footprint was larger than the one it thought it had. The staging environments were pulled offline, the retired subdomains were removed from DNS, and the patch-level disclosures were suppressed at the web server configuration level — all confirmed in a brief follow-up pass at no additional charge, since the fixes were infrastructure changes rather than application code.
